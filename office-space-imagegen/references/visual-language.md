# Visual language from the 67 office samples

This is the reusable visual synthesis of the user-provided image samples. Reference only the room imagery; ignore screenshot UI, captions, account names, comments, and other text. The samples are mostly polished modern workplace lounge, meeting, collaboration, and shared spaces. Open workstation layouts are an extension of their design language, not the dominant sampled use case.

## Shared visual DNA

- Modern, legible architectural structure combined with welcoming, home-like comfort.
- A considered balance of straight architectural lines (window grids, beams, glazing, service runs) and rounded elements (sofas, booths, rugs, pendants, niches).
- Several spatial layers: an immediate furniture/plant foreground, a functional central scene, and a window, glazed meeting room, work area, or service point in the background.
- Open circulation with local semi-private zones made by high-back seating, built-in niches, glass partitions, shelves, or low storage.
- One clear hero feature per image: e.g. a curved booth, distinctive ceiling light, color-blocked niche, sculptural column, or carefully designed workstation cluster. Supporting details should not compete.
- Carefully finished architectural visualization or interior photography: realistic scale and joins, legible materials, straight verticals, coherent lighting, moderate wide-angle perspective.

## Random style branches

Choose a branch randomly for each new set or vary the branch by image. Do not default to one branch across sessions. A requested palette/style overrides the random choice. Each image should use one main branch and at most one secondary influence.

1. **Natural light-industrial:** exposed concrete ceiling/slab, neatly arranged visible services, light wood, neutral seating, daylight. Airy and pragmatic, never distressed or ruin-like.
2. **Bright soft modern:** warm white ceiling or selected ceiling rafts, pale oak, powder blue and sage furniture, curved planters/booths, soft ring or disc lights. Spacious, calm, luminous.
3. **Energetic collaboration:** neutral shell with concentrated orange, yellow, blue, or blush accents; colored niches, modular seats, mobile tables, and graphic rugs. Use color in controlled areas.
4. **Refined dark:** forest green, navy, burgundy, charcoal, and walnut with warm localized lighting. Keep enough window/daylight balance; avoid a uniformly dark image.
5. **Sculptural nature:** restrained architecture, one expressive ring, woven, mesh, or geometric luminaire/overhead feature, with selective wood, glass, and plants.

Randomize color direction, ceiling treatment, lighting family, furniture grouping, plant placement, partitioning, and camera rhythm. Do not mix every branch in one room or repeat one sofa-table-planter formula across all six images.

## Palette and materials

The samples vary from light neutrals and pale oak to richer navy, green, orange, yellow, purple, and burgundy accents. Neutrals often provide the visual field while one or two accent colors identify a particular area. These are suggested directions, not measured sample values.

- Light options: warm white, pale grey, cream, pale oak, powder blue, blue-grey, soft sage.
- Energetic options: orange, mustard yellow, coral, blush, green, blue against warm grey/wood/white.
- Refined options: forest green, navy, burgundy, charcoal, walnut, warm ivory.
- Architectural surfaces: concrete, fine metal, white or grey floor, black/white slim-frame glass.
- Warm surfaces: oak/walnut veneers, wood tabletops, cabinet faces, and seat bases.
- Soft surfaces: upholstery, felt, rugs, textured fabric, controlled leather-like sheen.
- Special finishes such as stone, woven mesh, tile, or patterned wall surfacing should be localized.

Use layered material contrast; avoid making every surface glossy or every wall decorative. In light palettes, retain enough tonal contrast to show the furniture silhouette and circulation.

## Layout, furniture, and composition

- Desk/work areas: varied viable arrangements such as paired desks, benching, perimeter/window workstations, small focus rooms, or mixed team neighborhoods. Keep ergonomic task seating and clear aisles.
- Social areas: can use curved modular sofas, face-to-face lounge chairs, booths, window benches, café-height tables, or stepped seating. Pick a layout that suits the room rather than applying one generic sofa cluster.
- Meeting areas: select conference table form and seating count to fit the room. Show plausible access, glass privacy, technology/display integration where appropriate.
- Collaboration areas: consider movable project tables, shared tables, mobile whiteboards, standing-height settings, small breakout nooks, or reconfigurable lounge arrangements.
- Rugs can define a social island; align their shape with a round table, curved seating, or clear zone boundary. Do not cover every floor area.
- Use windows for daylight and spatial depth. A side window with a view down the office is a common effective composition, but vary camera position and orientation between images.
- Use a human-eye interior-photography viewpoint, moderate wide-angle field, corrected verticals, and show enough floor, ceiling, and furniture to explain the room. User-provided aspect ratio wins.

## Greenery: restrained accent only

Use typically one or two localized plant moments: a medium/tall potted plant near a window/corner and optionally a small tabletop or low planter grouping. Keep pots/planters visible and preserve negative space. Do not add dense layers, a full green wall, continuous planter beds, crowded shelves, or a ceiling full of hanging vines. Plants must not obscure Steelcase furniture or office function.

## Variety across the set

Generate exactly six distinct full-frame images, not a collage. Scene types are unrestricted and can repeat. Repeated functions must differ visibly in architectural organization, orientation, seating/workstation layout, circulation, partition strategy, window relationship, ceiling, hero feature, or camera. Vary several of these, not just accent color.

The set may contain separate projects with different architectural shells, or connected views of one office if the user asks for continuity. Only connected views need matching building structure, glazing, ceiling, floor, wood tone, and furnishings. In either case, common sample DNA and polished quality should hold.

## Avoid

- Repeating the same layout/camera and merely recoloring it.
- Treating six sample room types as a mandatory list.
- Plants as the main visual subject.
- Blocked aisles, impossible furniture scale, floating pieces, broken desk/chair geometry, inconsistent glazing, or implausible service runs.
- Overexposed windows, all-over yellow light, excessive glowing edges, distorted ultra-wide perspective, excessive ceiling spectacle.
- Making an office look like a residential living room or hotel lobby without visible workplace intent.
- Any screenshot borders, interface controls, labels, captions, logos, legible text, page numbers, or watermarks.
